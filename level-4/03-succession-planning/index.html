    03 · Succession Planning¶

    Succession planning is usually treated as an HR compliance exercise — a spreadsheet updated once a year with a name in the "ready now" column that nobody has told. Done as servant leadership, it is the opposite of that: a genuine act of care for the organization's future and for the people being developed toward it, done transparently rather than as a secret list. This module gives you a real succession framework, a matrix you can actually use, and how to have the conversations that make succession planning something done with people rather than about them behind their backs.

    1. Why the secret-list approach fails, structurally¶

    The traditional model — leadership privately identifies successors, tells no one, and reveals the choice only when a role opens — has two failure modes. First, the people not on the list never get the development investment that would have made them viable, because nobody told them there was a gap to close. Second, the people on the list often don't know it, so they leave for a role elsewhere that made the opportunity explicit, and the "succession plan" evaporates the moment it's tested. Transparency, done well, fixes both.

    2. The Succession Matrix¶

    SUCCESSION MATRIX — role: ________________  current holder: ________

READINESS AXIS (rows)              CANDIDATE AXIS (one row per person)
  Ready now
  Ready in 1-2 years (named gap)
  Ready in 3+ years (named gap)
  Not on a succession path (say why, honestly)

FOR EACH CANDIDATE:
  Name: ________  Current readiness: ________
  The ONE gap standing between them and ready-now:
  Development action addressing that gap (specific, not generic):
  Have they been told they're on this path? [ ] yes [ ] no
    (if no, why not — this needs a real answer)
  Retention risk if not given real signal in next 12 months:
    [ ] low [ ] medium [ ] high

BENCH DEPTH CHECK
  If the current holder left tomorrow with no notice, who
  covers, even imperfectly, for the first 90 days?
  ______________________________________________

SINGLE POINT OF FAILURE FLAG
  Any critical role with zero named candidates at any
  readiness level: ______________________________________

    The "have they been told" row is where most matrices fail in practice — filled in with "no" and no honest reason, which usually means the organization is treating succession as a private insurance policy rather than a development commitment to the person.

    3. Worked example: a transparent succession conversation¶

    Wei is the CFO. Her matrix names Anaya, a Senior Director of FP&A, as "ready in 1-2 years" for Wei's own role, with the named gap being board-facing communication experience. Wei has never told Anaya any of this.

    Wei: I want to have a conversation I should have had six months ago. You're on my succession matrix for this role — not the only name, but a real one — at "one to two years out." I haven't told you that until now, and I want to be honest about why: I was worried that naming it and then not delivering on it would be worse than saying nothing.

    Anaya: I... didn't expect that. What's actually standing between me and "ready now"?

    Wei: One specific thing: you've never presented to the board. Everything else — the technical depth, the team's respect for you — is already there. The board comfort only comes from doing it, repeatedly, under real scrutiny.

    Anaya: Is there a path to actually doing that, or is this a label with nothing behind it?

    Wei: That's the fair question, and here's the concrete plan: you present the Q3 forecast section at the next board meeting, with me in the room but not presenting it myself. If that goes well, you own the full FP&A section going forward, and I step back from it entirely within two quarters.

    Anaya: What if it doesn't go well?

    Wei: Then we debrief specifically — what part didn't land, and we try again in a lower-stakes forum first, like the audit committee. I'm not putting this on a shelf either way. What I need from you is honesty if this isn't actually the path you want — some people don't want this seat, and pretending otherwise wastes both our time.

    Anaya: No, I want it. I just didn't know it was real until right now.

    Wei converted a private label on a spreadsheet into a concrete, falsifiable commitment with a specific mechanism (the Q3 board presentation) and an honest failure path — the same discipline as a delegation contract, applied to a career-defining decision instead of a single task.

    4. Multiple candidates, and the cost of silence to the ones not chosen¶

    Naming more than one candidate for a role is healthy competition only if handled honestly — silence to the candidates who are further behind reads as exclusion once they discover, inevitably, that others were told something they weren't. The honest approach: tell every named candidate their actual position and gap, not just the frontrunner. "You are on this path, currently further out than one other person, and here's specifically what would close that distance" respects someone enough to let them decide whether to invest in closing the gap or pursue something else.

    5. Bench depth beyond the obvious successor¶

    Succession planning that only names the most likely single successor leaves the organization exposed if that person leaves before the transition. Build actual bench depth: a 90-day emergency cover option ( who could hold the role imperfectly while a longer search happens) is a different question from who the ideal long-term successor is, and both need an honest answer, not just the second one.

    6. Succession for your own role, specifically¶

    Leaders are often the worst at planning their own succession, either from insecurity (a strong successor implies replaceability) or genuine belief no one is ready. Treat your own succession with the same rigor as any other role in the matrix — if you cannot name at least one person on a real, disclosed development path toward your own seat, that is itself the single point of failure the matrix in section 2 is designed to surface, and it is the one leaders are most tempted to leave blank.

    Exercise¶

    Build the Succession Matrix in section 2 for at least two critical roles in your organization, including your own. For each named candidate, check the "have they been told" box honestly, and where it's "no," write the real reason — not a diplomatic one. Pick the candidate closest to ready and have the transparent conversation Wei had with Anaya within the next two weeks, including a concrete, falsifiable next step, not just an acknowledgment that they're "on the radar."
