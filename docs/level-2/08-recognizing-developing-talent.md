# 08 · Recognizing & Developing Talent

Most managers identify talent by noticing who reminds them of
themselves, and then develop it by sending that person on a course. Both
halves are wrong. The first produces a team that is demographically and
cognitively narrow and quietly demoralised; the second produces a
certificate and no change in behaviour. Recognising talent is a
discipline of looking for specific observable signals — several of which
look like inconvenience — and developing it is mostly about the work you
assign, not the training you buy. This module gives you a performance
and potential grid, a signal checklist, a development plan template
built on the 70/20/10 split, and a worked development conversation.

## 1. Performance is not potential

The most expensive confusion in talent management. Performance is
delivery in the current role; potential is capacity to succeed in a
materially different one. They correlate weakly, and treating them as
the same thing is how organisations promote their best individual
contributor into a mediocre manager and lose both.

|  | **Low potential signal** | **High potential signal** |
|---|---|---|
| **High performance** | **Core strength.** Excellent where they are. Deepen, reward, do not push upward against their wishes | **Grow.** Stretch now, or they leave within a year |
| **Low performance** | **Address.** Module 6 conversation — role fit or performance | **Investigate.** Almost always a fixable cause: wrong role, bad fit with you, unclear expectations, life circumstances |

Two notes on using this honestly. The "core strength" box is not a
consolation prize — a team of people brilliant and content in their
current roles is a functional team, and pressuring them into management
because it is the only visible progression is a failure of your career
ladder, not of them. And the "investigate" box is where most overlooked
talent sits; low performance is a symptom with many causes, and only one
of them is capability.

## 2. Signals of potential you can actually observe

Not "shows leadership." These are behaviours you can point to with a
date attached.

| Signal | What it looks like concretely | Why it predicts |
|---|---|---|
| Learning agility | Changes their approach after feedback within weeks, not months | The single strongest predictor of success in an unfamiliar role |
| Asks about the why | Wants the business reason behind a task, not just the task | Will make good decisions when you're not there |
| Seeks disconfirmation | Goes and finds out where they're wrong; changes their mind out loud | Won't defend a failing plan to protect their ego |
| Scope creep, the good kind | Notices adjacent problems and fixes them unasked | Already operating a level up |
| Teaches | Others go to them, informally, and leave abler | Multiplier, not just a producer |
| Handles ambiguity | Makes progress with incomplete information rather than waiting | Senior roles are mostly incomplete information |
| Takes the unglamorous job | Volunteers for the necessary, unrewarded thing | Ownership rather than positioning |

And the signals that mislead, which is the harder half of this list:

- **Confidence.** Reads as potential and predicts almost nothing.
- **Visibility.** Being in the room with you is a function of geography
  and personality. Some of your strongest people are on a different
  site, in a different timezone, or simply quiet.
- **Similarity to you.** The most reliably distorting bias in this whole
  area. If your list of high-potentials all share your background,
  communication style or working pattern, the list is wrong.
- **Long hours.** Measures availability, not capability, and frequently
  measures poor delegation.

A cheap corrective: for each direct report, write down one instance of a
potential signal from the table above with a date. Whoever you cannot
fill in is not necessarily low-potential — they may just be someone you
have not been watching.

## 3. The development plan

Development is 70% challenging work, 20% people, 10% formal learning.
Most plans invert this, because the 10% is the only part with a budget
code. Build the plan from the assignment backwards.

```text
DEVELOPMENT PLAN
Person: ______________  Period: __________  Reviewed: __________

WHERE THEY WANT TO GO
  In their words (not my paraphrase):
  What they want that I know the organisation can't give:

STRENGTH TO DEEPEN
  The thing they are unusually good at:
  How the next 6 months makes them more so:

THE ONE GAP THAT MATTERS
  (Only one. A plan with four gaps develops nothing)
  The gap, stated behaviourally:
  What "closed" would look like, observably:

70 · THE WORK
  Stretch assignment that requires the gap to close:
  What could genuinely go wrong, and what I'll do about it:
  Decision rights (see Module 5 level 1-7):  ____

20 · THE PEOPLE
  Who they should be exposed to, and how I'll arrange it:
  Who mentors, and on what specifically:
  What I will do to make their work visible upward:

10 · FORMAL
  Course/reading/certification, if any — and what it's FOR:

CHECKPOINTS
  Dates:                Format:
  How we'll know it worked (observable, not "feels better"):
```

The "what could go wrong" box is the difference between a stretch
assignment and a trap. A stretch that fails publicly with no cover from
you is not development; it is an expensive lesson in not volunteering.

## 4. A worked development conversation

Hana manages a customer support function. Joel is her steadiest agent —
high performance, three years in role, never puts himself forward,
regularly ignored in talent reviews because he is quiet and works from a
regional office. Hana has noticed three signals: new joiners informally
go to him, he rewrote the refund guidance nobody asked him to touch, and
he changed his approach to escalations within two weeks of one piece of
feedback.

> **Hana:** I want to talk about the next couple of years rather than
> this month's numbers. Where do you actually want to go? Not the
> version you think I want to hear.
>
> **Joel:** Honestly, I don't know. I've been asked if I want to manage
> and I don't think I do. Which I assume means I'm stuck.
>
> **Hana:** It doesn't, and I want to separate two things. Do you not
> want to manage, or do you not want to stop doing the work?
>
> **Joel:** The second one. The managers here spend all day in meetings
> about the work and never touch it. That looks miserable to me.
>
> **Hana:** That's useful, because there's a direction that isn't
> management. Can I tell you what I've actually observed? Three things.
> Every new starter in the last year has ended up asking you things
> rather than their buddy. You rewrote the refund guidance in March
> without being asked, and it's the version everyone uses now. And after
> we talked about escalation tone in February, your escalations changed
> inside two weeks — that one is rarer than you'd think.
>
> **Joel:** I didn't think anyone noticed the refund thing.
>
> **Hana:** It's the reason we're having this conversation. So the
> strength is that you make other people better at the work, and you
> improve the work itself. The gap — and there's only one I care about
> — is that all of that happens in a five-metre radius around your desk.
> You've never presented anything, never sat in a cross-functional
> forum, and nobody outside this office knows your name. That's what
> stops you being a principal-level specialist rather than a senior
> agent.
>
> **Joel:** That's fair. The presenting part is genuinely unappealing.
>
> **Hana:** Then let's make it as small as possible and real rather than
> practising. Here's what I'd propose for the next six months. The work:
> you own the redesign of the whole escalation playbook — that's your
> call at level 5, meaning you decide and I back it, and it needs sign
> off from Ops and Legal, which means you'll have to explain it to
> people who don't work here. The people: I'll introduce you to Sanjay
> in Ops, and I'd like Priya to mentor you on one specific thing, which
> is how she runs a room she doesn't chair. And I'll do this — every
> time the playbook comes up in my director's meeting, I'll say it's
> yours and I'll have you present it once, in the last month, not the
> first.
>
> **Joel:** What happens if Legal blocks the whole thing?
>
> **Hana:** Then we do it together and I say publicly it was my scoping
> that missed the constraint, because that will be true. What I won't do
> is take it back off you halfway.
>
> **Joel:** What's the formal bit? There's usually a course.
>
> **Hana:** Only if you want one, and only for something specific.
> There's a half-day on facilitating workshops that would actually be
> used in month four. That's it — I'm not sending you on anything to
> tick a box.

Note what makes this a plan rather than a chat: one gap, an assignment
that cannot be completed without closing it, a named delegation level, a
named mentor with a specific subject, an explicit commitment about
visibility, and a stated failure position.

## 5. Failure modes

- **The nine-box as a verdict.** Grids are for prompting a conversation
  about a person, not for stamping them. Position changes with role,
  manager and life stage; re-examine at least annually.
- **Development plans nobody re-reads.** A plan with no checkpoint date
  is a document, not a plan. Put the review in both calendars now.
- **Developing only the visible.** Set yourself the test in section 2 —
  one dated signal per person. The blanks are your blind spots.
- **Promising what you can't deliver.** Never imply a promotion you do
  not control. Say what you will do — advocate, assign, make visible —
  and be explicit about what is not yours to give.

## Exercise

Place every direct report on the grid in section 1, then justify each
placement with one dated observation from the table in section 2. Any
placement you cannot evidence is an impression, not an assessment — mark
those, because they are where your biases live.

Then pick one person, ideally from the "investigate" box or someone you
rarely see, and build the plan in section 3 with them — in a
conversation, not beforehand. Hold yourself to two constraints: exactly
one gap, and the 70% assignment must be work that already needs doing,
not an exercise invented for their benefit. Book the first checkpoint
before you leave the room.
