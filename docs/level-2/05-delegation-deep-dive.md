# 05 · Delegation Deep Dive

Level 1 covered handing over a task. This module covers the two things
that actually limit a leader's capacity: handing over a *decision*, and
refusing to absorb conflict that belongs to someone else. Most managers
who believe they delegate well are delegating execution and retaining
judgement — the report does the work, you approve the outcome, and your
calendar stays full while nobody grows past the approval step. The
symptom is a manager who cannot take two consecutive weeks off. This
module gives you a seven-level delegation scale, a written delegation
contract, a rule for handling escalated conflict, and two worked
examples.

## 1. Seven levels of delegation

"Delegate more" is useless advice because delegation is not binary.
Naming the level converts a vague handover into a shared expectation.

| Level | Who decides | You say |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Tell | You decide, they execute | "Here's the decision and why." |
| 2 · Sell | You decide, and you sell it | "Here's the decision; let me explain the reasoning so you can carry it." |
| 3 · Consult | You decide, after their input | "I want your view before I decide. The call is mine." |
| 4 · Agree | You decide together | "Neither of us decides alone; we land it jointly." |
| 5 · Advise | They decide, with your input | "My view is X. It's your call, and I'll back whichever way you go." |
| 6 · Inquire | They decide, then tell you | "You decide. Tell me what you chose and why, afterwards." |
| 7 · Delegate | They decide; you don't need to know | "It's yours. I don't need to be in it at all." |

Three rules that make this usable:

- **State the level out loud, at the start.** The single most common
  delegation failure is a level mismatch: you thought level 6, they
  thought level 3, so they wait for you and you think they're passive.
- **Level per decision, not per person.** A report can be at 7 on
  supplier scheduling and 3 on anything touching a regulator, at the
  same time, and that is coherent rather than inconsistent.
- **Move one level at a time, and say when.** "You're at 5 on this now;
  after the next two, I want you at 6" turns delegation into a visible
  development path instead of an unpredictable weather system.

## 2. The delegation contract

For anything meaningful, five minutes writing this down prevents weeks
of drift. Fill it in with the person, not for them.

```text
DELEGATION CONTRACT
Decision or area: ______________________  Owner: ______________
Level (1-7): ____   Review date: __________

OUTCOME
  What good looks like (in observable terms):
  What is explicitly NOT in scope:

CONSTRAINTS  (the real boundaries — be exhaustive, they can't guess)
  Budget ceiling:
  People who must be consulted:
  Deadlines that are immovable:
  Decisions that must come back to me, and why:

INFORMATION
  What I know that they don't (context, history, politics):
  Who has the rest of it:

AUTHORITY
  What they can commit on my behalf:
  How they should describe their mandate to others:
  What I will say publicly to back that mandate:

CHECK-INS
  Cadence:                        Format:
  What I will do at a check-in:  (review progress / answer questions)
  What I will NOT do:            (take the decision back)

FAILURE
  What happens if it goes wrong — what I will do, in advance:
```

The last box matters more than the rest. If you have not decided in
advance how you will behave when a delegated decision goes badly, you
will behave the way you feel in the moment, which is to take it back —
and the team will read the reversal, not the original delegation.

## 3. Delegating a decision, not just the work

The test of whether you delegated a decision is simple: could they make
a choice you would not have made, and would it stand? If the answer is
no, you delegated a recommendation.

> Ana runs a 40-person operations function. She has told Priyanka to
> "own" the choice of a new shift-scheduling vendor. Two weeks later
> Priyanka arrives with a comparison and no decision.

> **Priyanka:** Here are the three. Which do you want?
>
> **Ana:** I said this was yours, and then I never said at what level,
> which is my mistake. Let me fix that now. This is level 5 — you
> decide, I'll give you my view if you want it, and I'll back whichever
> way you go, including if it's not the one I'd pick.
>
> **Priyanka:** ...Okay. Do you want to give me the view?
>
> **Ana:** Do you want it before or after you tell me yours?
>
> **Priyanka:** After. Fine. I'd take Meridian. It's 15% more expensive
> and the interface is worse, but they'll do the data migration and the
> other two won't, and we don't have anyone to do a migration in Q3.
>
> **Ana:** My instinct was the cheapest one, because I was looking at
> the annual cost and not at who does the migration. You've weighted a
> constraint I'd underweighted. Go with Meridian.
>
> **Priyanka:** What if the migration slips and it looks bad?
>
> **Ana:** Then it's a decision I backed. Here's what I'll actually do:
> if procurement or Finance push back, I'll tell them the choice was
> yours and that I support it — I won't distance myself. What I'd like
> in return is that you tell me early if it's going wrong, not late.
> Constraints I should have given you at the start: the ceiling is 90k a
> year, Legal has to see any contract over two years, and don't sign
> anything past March without me because of the budget cycle.
>
> **Priyanka:** That's the first time I've had the actual number.

Note the two repairs. Ana names the level explicitly, and she states the
constraints she had been holding in her head — the most common reason
delegated decisions come back is that the person is guessing at
boundaries nobody told them, and guessing conservatively means
escalating.

## 4. Delegating upward conflict back down

The other capacity drain is being used as a channel between two people
who could talk to each other. It feels like support and it is a habit
you are training.

The rule: **you carry the relationship, they carry the conversation.**
Absorb the conflict only when there is a genuine power asymmetry, when
you have information they don't, or when it has already failed at their
level twice.

> **Dan:** Can you talk to Finance? They keep rejecting our purchase
> requests and Marta's impossible.
>
> **Ana:** Tell me what's actually happening. What got rejected?
>
> **Dan:** Three requests this month. The reason field just says
> "insufficient detail."
>
> **Ana:** What did Marta say when you asked what detail she needs?
>
> **Dan:** I haven't. She's rejected me three times, I'm not going to
> get a straight answer.
>
> **Ana:** So the state of play is: three rejections, one hypothesis
> about her, and no conversation. If I go and ask her, what happens the
> fourth time it gets rejected?
>
> **Dan:** ...I come back to you.
>
> **Ana:** Right, and you've learned that the route to Finance is
> through me, which is worse for you than for me. Here's what I'll do
> and won't do. I won't have the conversation. I will tell you two
> things I know that you don't: Marta's team got audited in February and
> they're under instruction to document justification on everything, and
> she is not the one who wrote that rule. And I'll message her today
> saying you'll be in touch and that you have my backing on the spend.
> You ask her one question: "What would a request look like that you
> could approve first time?"
>
> **Dan:** And if that goes nowhere?
>
> **Ana:** Then come back and we look at it again — with what she said.
> If it's still stuck after you've tried properly, I'll go with you, not
> instead of you.

Ana gave Dan exactly what he lacked (context and air cover) and withheld
exactly what he didn't (the conversation itself). She also set the
condition for escalation in advance, so coming back isn't a defeat.

## 5. Failure modes

- **Delegating the task, keeping the judgement.** "Draft it and I'll
  review" every time is level 1 wearing a level 5 costume.
- **Taking it back on the first wobble.** If you reverse a delegated
  decision, say why in terms of the contract — "this crossed a
  constraint I gave you" — not in terms of quality, or nobody will use
  their authority again.
- **Delegating without the information.** People escalate because they
  are guessing at boundaries. Write the constraints down.
- **Dumping.** Handing over work you find dull with no growth in it and
  calling it development is transparent, and it is noticed.

## Exercise

List every decision you personally made in the last two weeks. For each,
write the level from section 1 that you actually operated at, and the
level it *could* be at with the right constraints written down. Pick the
one with the biggest gap and fill in the delegation contract in section 2
with that person this week — including the failure box.

Separately, count the times in the last month you were the messenger
between two people who could have spoken directly. For the next one,
use Ana's structure in section 4: give the context and the air cover,
decline the conversation, and name in advance what would make escalation
legitimate.
