# 07 · Leading Through Change

Reorganisations, new systems, budget cuts, a merged team, a strategy
pivot — the announcement takes ten minutes and the disruption takes six
months. Most managers treat the gap between those two numbers as an
inconvenience to be minimised with enthusiasm. It isn't: it is the
actual work. Your team is not resisting the org chart, they are
processing the loss of a way of working that they were good at, and
enthusiasm aimed at people who are grieving competence reads as
contempt. This module gives you the transition model that explains the
gap, a communication plan template, a script for the announcement, and a
worked example of the conversation that follows it.

## 1. Change is external; transition is internal

William Bridges' distinction is the most practically useful idea in this
whole area. **Change** is the event: the new structure exists from the
1st. **Transition** is the psychological process people go through to
let go of the old and commit to the new, and it starts *later* than the
change and finishes much later.

| Phase | What people feel | What they do | What they need from you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ending** | Loss, anger, denial; grief for competence and status | Relitigate the decision; talk about how it used to work | Acknowledge the loss by name. Say what is actually ending, and what isn't |
| **Neutral zone** | Confusion, low productivity, anxiety, some creativity | Ask the same questions repeatedly; over-interpret small signals | Short-term goals, high-frequency communication, tolerance for the dip |
| **New beginning** | Cautious energy; identification with the new way | Take initiative; stop referencing the old structure | Quick wins made visible, and the new purpose stated in their terms |

Three consequences that follow directly:

- **The dip is real and is not a morale problem.** Expect reduced output
  in the neutral zone and plan for it rather than pushing harder into it.
- **You are ahead of them, always.** You have known about this for six
  weeks. Day one for them is week six for you, and the most common
  leadership failure in change is impatience born of forgetting that.
- **You cannot skip the ending.** A leader who opens with "this is a
  huge opportunity" before naming what's being lost is asking people to
  begin before they have finished, and they will simply not follow.

## 2. The four questions everyone is actually asking

Whatever the strategic rationale, every individual is running four
questions, in this order, and will not hear anything else until they are
answered.

1. **What happens to me?** Role, manager, team, location, pay.
2. **Why is this happening?** In plain terms, not strategy-deck terms.
3. **When will I know?** A date, even if the date is "I'll know by the
   14th and will tell you the same day."
4. **What is staying the same?** Continuity is what makes change
   survivable, and nobody ever mentions it.

Question 4 is the one leaders omit entirely. "Your team, your clients
and your manager are unchanged; what changes is who approves spend" cuts
the perceived size of a change in half, truthfully.

## 3. Change communication plan

```text
CHANGE COMMUNICATION PLAN
Change: ______________________  Announced: ______  Effective: ______

WHAT IS ENDING
  Ways of working that stop:
  What people will lose (status, expertise, relationships, autonomy):
  Who loses the most, by name:
  What I will say explicitly about that loss:

WHAT IS NOT CHANGING
  (list at least three concrete things)

THE FOUR QUESTIONS — my answer to each, in one sentence
  What happens to me:
  Why:
  When will I know:
  What stays the same:

WHAT I DON'T KNOW YET
  Open questions:
  The date by which I'll have each answer:
  (Never fill this section with "nothing" — nobody believes it)

SEQUENCE
  Told individually first (most affected):
  Told in team meeting:
  Told by someone else / broadcast:
  Order and timing:  (nobody hears it first from a group email)

CADENCE THROUGH THE NEUTRAL ZONE
  Update rhythm:                 (weekly minimum, even with no news)
  Standing "no news this week" message: yes / no
  Short-term goal for the next 4 weeks:
  What quick win will I make visible, and when:
```

The "what I don't know yet" box is what buys you credibility. A manager
who admits three open questions and then answers them on the promised
date is trusted; a manager who projects total certainty is discounted
the first time reality diverges.

## 4. The announcement

Marisa leads a nine-person market research team. Following a merger, the
team is being split: four people move into a central insights function
under a different director, five stay with her doing a narrower brief.
She has known for five weeks. She has already spoken to the four movers
individually, the day before. This is the team meeting.

> **Marisa:** I'm going to tell you the decision, then what I know, then
> what I don't, and then we'll have as long as you want for questions.
>
> **Marisa:** From the 1st of next month, market research splits. Nikhil,
> Aisha, Tom and Ben move into the central insights function reporting
> to Deepa. Sara, Ravi, Lena, Marco and I stay in the commercial team
> and our brief narrows to pricing and product research. I spoke to
> everyone moving yesterday, so nobody in this room is hearing their own
> situation for the first time right now.
>
> **Marisa:** Before the rationale, let me say what's ending, because I
> think it's the bigger thing. This team has worked together for three
> years and we are unusually good at handing work between us — that
> specific thing, the way we cover each other, does not survive this. I
> don't want to pretend otherwise or call it an opportunity. It's a real
> loss and I'm sorry about it.
>
> **Marisa:** The why: the merged organisation has three research teams
> asking the same panels the same questions, and the duplication is
> genuinely costing money. I argued for keeping the team whole and I
> lost that argument. I still think the central function is the right
> call overall, and I want to be honest that both of those are true.
>
> **Marisa:** What isn't changing: no roles are at risk and no pay is
> changing in this reorg. Everyone keeps their current projects to
> completion. Our Tuesday session stays, for everyone, for at least six
> months, and I'll keep running it.
>
> **Marisa:** What I don't know: where the four of you will sit
> physically, whether the panel budget follows you, and what happens to
> the retail tracker after Q3. I'll have the first two by the 14th and
> I'll tell you the day I know, including if the answer is still no
> answer. The tracker I genuinely don't know and won't pretend to.
>
> **Aisha:** Is this the first stage of something bigger?
>
> **Marisa:** I don't know, and I won't tell you it isn't. What I can
> tell you is what I've been told, which is that there is no second
> phase planned this year, and that I'll say so plainly if that changes.
>
> **Ravi:** Do we get a say in who goes where?
>
> **Marisa:** No — that's decided. I'd rather tell you that than run a
> consultation that isn't one.

## 5. The neutral zone conversation

Three weeks later. Tom, one of the movers, is visibly disengaged.

> **Marisa:** How are you finding it?
>
> **Tom:** Fine. It's fine.
>
> **Marisa:** I'll go first, then. Three weeks in, I'm finding it worse
> than I expected. I keep starting to ask you something and remembering
> it's not mine to ask.
>
> **Tom:** ...Honestly, I don't know what I'm for at the moment. I was
> the person who knew the retail panel end to end. In the central team
> there are two other people who know it, and one of them knows it
> better. So what am I bringing?
>
> **Marisa:** That's the actual thing, isn't it. Not the reporting line
> — the expertise not being scarce any more.
>
> **Tom:** Yeah. I sound pathetic.
>
> **Marisa:** You sound like someone who lost the thing that made him
> valuable, three weeks ago. That's the normal middle of this and it
> doesn't mean it stays this way. Can I ask a different question — what
> do you know that those two people don't?
>
> **Tom:** Commercial context. They've never sat with a pricing team.
> They can run the panel; they can't tell you which question the
> business will act on.
>
> **Marisa:** That's a real and rare thing, and it's the bridge between
> the two teams. What would it take for Deepa to know you have it?
>
> **Tom:** She doesn't. I've been quiet in her meetings.
>
> **Marisa:** What's one thing in the next two weeks?
>
> **Tom:** There's a brief going out on the loyalty study. I could
> rewrite the question set from a commercial angle and send it to her
> with the reasoning.
>
> **Marisa:** Do that, and copy me — not to approve it, just so I can
> tell Deepa what you're bringing. I'll do that this week either way.

Marisa did not reassure, minimise, or sell the change. She named the
loss precisely (scarcity of expertise, not the reporting line), gave a
short-term goal, and used her position to make him visible. That is the
whole job in the neutral zone.

## 6. Failure modes

- **Announcing to a group before telling the most affected.** Nobody
  should hear their own fate in a room of nine people.
- **Relentless positivity.** Enthusiasm without acknowledged loss reads
  as either dishonest or oblivious, and costs you the trust you need
  later.
- **Going quiet when there's no news.** Silence gets filled with the
  worst available theory. A weekly "nothing new, next update Friday" is
  a real communication.
- **Pretending you agreed.** If you argued and lost, you may say so
  without dissent — "I argued for X, I lost, and I'm implementing this
  properly" is honest and loyal at the same time. What you may not do is
  privately disown it while publicly delivering it.

## Exercise

Take a change your team is living through now, or one you know is
coming, and fill in the plan in section 3 — especially "what is ending"
and "who loses the most, by name." For each named person, write the
specific thing they are losing: expertise that stops being scarce, a
relationship, autonomy, an identity as the person who knows X.

Then have one conversation this week with the person who loses the most,
using Marisa's structure in section 5: go first with something honest,
name the loss precisely rather than generally, agree one short-term
concrete goal for the next fortnight, and commit to one thing you will
do with your own visibility to help them.
