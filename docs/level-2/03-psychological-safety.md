# 03 · Building Psychological Safety

Ask any team whether people can speak up freely and almost everyone says
yes. Then watch what actually happens: the quality problem that was
obvious to three people in the room and mentioned by none, the "any
concerns?" that closes in four seconds, the mistake that gets quietly
patched rather than reported. Psychological safety is the shared belief
that you can say a hard thing here without paying for it socially — and
it is not a mood, it is a pattern of what has visibly happened to the
last few people who tried. This module gives you a diagnostic you can
run this month, a script for the ten seconds that matter most, and a
worked example of a leader getting it right and nearly getting it wrong.

## 1. What psychological safety is not

It is the most misread idea in modern management, usually in one of two
directions.

| Misreading | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| "Being nice to each other" | People can disagree sharply *because* the relationship survives it |
| "Lowering the bar" | Standards are unchanged; the fear of reporting a miss is what drops |
| "No negative feedback" | Feedback is more direct, because it isn't stored up and avoided |
| "Everyone agrees" | Dissent is visible early, when it's still cheap |
| "A trait of the people" | A property of the local climate — the same person is safe on one team and silent on another |

Amy Edmondson's most useful contribution is the pairing with
accountability, which is where the "lowering the bar" fear gets settled:

|  | **Low accountability** | **High accountability** |
|---|---|---|
| **High safety** | Comfort zone — pleasant, unproductive | **Learning zone** — hard goals, honest reporting |
| **Low safety** | Apathy zone — people are checked out | Anxiety zone — hard goals, hidden problems |

Most struggling teams are in the anxiety zone, and most managers try to
fix them by raising accountability further. That is the one move that
makes it worse.

## 2. A diagnostic you can actually run

Behaviour, not feelings. Ask these seven statements as a 1-5 agreement
scale, anonymously, and read the *spread* rather than the average — one
person at 1 tells you more than a mean of 3.9 does.

```text
TEAM CLIMATE CHECK  (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree)

 1. If I make a mistake on this team, it is not held against me.
 2. People on this team are able to raise problems and tough issues.
 3. People here sometimes reject others for being different.        (reverse)
 4. It is safe to take a risk on this team.
 5. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help.     (reverse)
 6. Nobody here would deliberately act to undermine my efforts.
 7. My unique skills and talents are valued and made use of.

Free text: "Think of something you didn't say in the last month
that you now wish you had. What stopped you?"
```

The free-text question does most of the work. If the answers cluster
around one named person, you have a behaviour problem to address
directly. If they cluster around "it wouldn't have changed anything,"
you have a *futility* problem rather than a fear problem, and the fix is
showing that input changes outcomes — different disease, different cure.

Behavioural indicators that need no survey at all:

- Bad news reaches you late, or from outside the team.
- The same two people speak in every meeting.
- Nobody has disagreed with you out loud in a month.
- Mistakes get described in the passive voice: "the file got sent."
- People pre-clear opinions with you in a DM before saying them in a
  meeting.

## 3. The ten seconds after someone speaks up

Safety is built almost entirely in the moments immediately following a
risky disclosure, because everyone else in the room is watching what it
costs. This is the whole intervention, and it fits on a card.

| Move | Say | Never say |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Thank the disclosure before evaluating the content | "Thank you for flagging that — I'd rather know now." | "How did that happen?" as your first sentence |
| 2. Separate person from event | "What in the process let this get through?" | "Why did *you* miss it?" |
| 3. Own your part | "I set that deadline. That's on me too." | Silence about your own contribution |
| 4. Ask for more, not less | "What else is in that category that I don't know about?" | "Let's take this offline." |
| 5. Convert it to a visible change | "Here's what we're changing, and I'll say so in Friday's update." | Nothing changes, and everyone notes it |

Step 5 is the one leaders skip. A team that reports a problem, sees no
change, and is then asked to report more problems learns quickly that
speaking up is decorative.

## 4. Three framing moves that create it up front

- **Frame the work as a learning problem, not an execution problem.**
  "We have never done this before, so I expect us to be wrong about some
  of it — I want to be wrong early and cheaply" gives people advance
  permission for the exact disclosure you need.
- **Acknowledge your own fallibility specifically.** Not "I don't have
  all the answers," which is a platitude, but "I was wrong about the
  November forecast — I anchored on last year and ignored what two of
  you told me."
- **Ask questions that presume a problem exists.** "Any concerns?"
  invites silence. "What's the most likely way this goes wrong?" and
  "What am I not seeing?" invite content, because they make speaking up
  the compliant answer rather than the brave one.

## 5. A worked example

Elena leads a twelve-person client services team. In a weekly team
meeting, Nadia — six weeks into the job — says something unplanned.

> **Nadia:** Um. Before we move on. The Harding invoice that went out
> Tuesday had last quarter's rate on it. They've already queried it.
>
> **Elena:** Thank you for saying that out loud in the meeting rather
> than sorting it out quietly. Genuinely — I'd rather know now.
>
> *(Ten seconds pass. Elena does not ask how it happened.)*
>
> **Elena:** So let's do the useful version of this. What in the process
> let an old rate get onto a live invoice?
>
> **Nadia:** The rate card in the shared folder is called "rates final
> v2." There's a "rates 2024 final" in the same folder. I opened the
> wrong one.
>
> **Marcus:** I've done that. I caught it, so I never mentioned it.
>
> **Elena:** That's the part I want to name — Marcus caught his, so it
> stayed invisible, and Nadia's was the one that got through. That's
> luck, not competence. And the folder is mine; I built it, and I never
> archived the old card. That's on me.
>
> **Elena:** What else is in this category that I don't know about?
>
> **Priya:** The client contact list has the same problem. Two versions,
> nobody knows which is live.
>
> **Elena:** Right. This week: I archive every superseded file into a
> dated folder, and the live rate card gets one name and one location.
> Nadia, will you draft the reply to Harding and send it to me before it
> goes? Not because I don't trust it — because it's your first client
> correction and you shouldn't have to guess at the tone.
>
> **Nadia:** Yes. I've got a draft already.
>
> **Elena:** I'll mention this in Friday's update to the wider team,
> including the folder being my mistake. The point isn't the invoice,
> it's that we found it in three days instead of at quarter end.

Compare the version where Elena's first sentence is "How did that
happen?" — a fair question, asked at the wrong second. Nadia explains
defensively, Marcus never mentions his near-miss, Priya says nothing
about the contact list, and the folder problem survives to catch the
next new joiner. The content of the meeting is identical. What differs
is which sentence came first.

## 6. Where it breaks

- **Safety for some.** Climate is often uneven along lines of tenure,
  seniority, language, or being the only person like themselves in the
  room. An average score can hide a person sitting at 1. Read the spread.
- **Confusing safety with consensus.** You still make calls people
  dislike. Safety means they can say so first, and that you say plainly
  it was your call and why — not that the call changes.
- **Performance masquerading as safety.** Someone repeatedly missing
  agreed commitments is not a safety issue and should not be handled as
  one. That is Module 6.

## Exercise

Run the seven-statement check in section 2 with your team this month,
anonymously, including the free-text question. Before you read the
results, write down your predicted average for each item — the gap
between your prediction and the result is the more useful number.

Then do the harder half. Recall the last three times someone brought you
bad news. Write down your literal first sentence each time, as best you
remember it, and compare it against the table in section 3. If your
first sentence was a question about cause rather than a thank-you,
rehearse the replacement out loud now, so that it's available when you
next need it under pressure.
