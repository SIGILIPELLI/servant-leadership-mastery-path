# 09 · Servant Leadership in Meetings

Meetings are where your leadership is most visible and least examined.
Everything from Levels 1 and 2 — listening, safety, conflict, coaching —
either shows up in the room or is theoretical. The default pattern is
well known: the most senior person states a view first, the same two
people speak, everyone else calibrates to what has already been said,
and the meeting produces agreement that dissolves the moment people
leave. Serving the group's thinking means designing the meeting so the
information in the quietest heads reaches the decision. This module gives
you a meeting design canvas, a facilitation move set, and a worked
before-and-after of the same meeting run two ways.

## 1. One meeting, one job

Most bad meetings are two or three meetings wearing a trenchcoat. Name
the type before you write the agenda, and never run two types in one
block without a hard break between them.

| Type | Output | Right format | Fatal mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inform | Shared understanding | Written first, meeting only for questions | Reading slides aloud to people who can read |
| Generate | A wide set of options | Silent generation, then build | Starting with open discussion — first idea anchors everything |
| Decide | A named decision with an owner and date | Options pre-circulated, decision rights stated up front | Discovering mid-meeting that nobody knows who decides |
| Build | Alignment and commitment | Small group, real work done in the room | Inviting an audience |
| Connect | Relationship and trust | Unstructured, protected time | Adding an agenda and killing the point |

The single highest-value question to ask before any meeting you call:
*what will be true at the end that isn't true now?* If you can't answer
it in one sentence, cancel and send a note.

## 2. The meeting design canvas

```text
MEETING DESIGN CANVAS

Type (one only):  inform / generate / decide / build / connect
What is true at the end that isn't true now:
  ____________________________________________

DECISION RIGHTS  (state these out loud at the start)
  Who decides:
  Who must be consulted (their input changes the outcome):
  Who is informed (present for context, not for a vote):
  Is this actually already decided?  yes / no
    If yes, this is an INFORM meeting. Say so and don't fake a debate.

ATTENDEES
  Name — why they are here — what happens if they aren't:
  (No answer to column three = remove them and send the notes)

PRE-WORK
  What is circulated, and when (24h minimum):
  What people should arrive having done:

DESIGN
  First 5 minutes:            (silent read / silent write / round)
  How the quiet people get airtime:
  When I speak:               (target: last, on any question I own)
  How dissent is invited:     (specific mechanism, not "any concerns?")

CLOSE
  Decision, owner, date — read aloud before anyone leaves:
  What I will send in writing, and by when:
  Who was not in the room and needs to hear it from me directly:
```

## 3. Facilitation moves

Techniques with mechanisms, not exhortations.

| Move | How | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|
| Speak last | Withhold your view until everyone with a view has given theirs | Anchoring on the senior voice — the biggest single distortion in the room |
| Silent start | Five minutes reading the doc or writing options, in the room, no talking | Meetings where only the fast talkers and the pre-prepared contribute |
| Brainwriting | Everyone writes three options privately before any discussion | First-idea anchoring in generate meetings |
| Round | Go round the table by name, everyone says one thing, no cross-talk | The same two people occupying 80% of airtime |
| Named dissent | "I want the strongest argument against this — Ravi, you go first" | Groupthink; makes objecting the assigned task rather than a risky act |
| Airtime count | Tally who spoke, privately, for one meeting | Your own inaccurate sense of how balanced the discussion was |
| Process, not content | "We've been on this ten minutes with no new information — decide or park?" | Meetings that drift and end at the time limit rather than at an answer |
| Read-back close | State decision, owner and date aloud before anyone leaves | Meetings that "agreed" and produce nothing |

**Speak last is the whole module in one habit.** If you state your view
first, the rest of the meeting is a referendum on you, and you will
mistake compliance for consensus.

## 4. Same meeting, two ways

A decision meeting: whether to move customer onboarding from the sales
team to the support team. Convener is Dara, the director. Present: Ines
(sales lead), Kwame (support lead), Bea (ops analyst), Tunde (new
regional manager, four weeks in).

**Version one, the default**

> **Dara:** Thanks all. So — I've looked at this and I think onboarding
> clearly belongs in support. Sales shouldn't be doing implementation.
> Thoughts?
>
> **Kwame:** Agreed, makes sense.
>
> **Ines:** I mean — if that's the direction, we'd need headcount to
> move with it, but yes, I can see it.
>
> **Dara:** Bea?
>
> **Bea:** No objections from me.
>
> **Dara:** Tunde?
>
> **Tunde:** Happy to go with the room.
>
> **Dara:** Great, that was easy. Let's do it.

Eight minutes, unanimous, and worthless. Dara anchored in sentence one,
so nobody after her was giving a view — they were responding to hers.
Bea has churn data showing onboarding quality drives first-year renewal.
Tunde has watched this exact move fail at his previous employer. Neither
piece of information reached the decision.

**Version two, designed**

> **Dara:** Decision today: where onboarding sits from Q3. To be clear
> up front about rights — I decide, but Ines and Kwame's input changes
> the outcome because it's their headcount, and Bea and Tunde are here
> because they know things I don't. This is not pre-decided; if it were
> I'd tell you.
>
> **Dara:** Five minutes, silently, on the doc I sent. Write down the
> single strongest argument for each option, including the one you don't
> favour. Then we'll go round.
>
> *(Five minutes.)*
>
> **Dara:** Round. Tunde first — you're newest, so you're least
> contaminated by how we've always done it.
>
> **Tunde:** At my last company we made this exact move. It failed, and
> the reason was specific: support was measured on ticket closure time,
> and onboarding is slow work. Within two quarters they'd deprioritised
> it into nothing. The structure wasn't the problem, the metric was.
>
> **Bea:** Mine's the other direction. Accounts with an onboarding
> quality score above 7 renew at 91%; below 4, it's 62%. That's the
> largest single lever in the renewal number, and right now nobody owns
> that score.
>
> **Kwame:** My argument for taking it: my team already picks up the
> failures at month three, blind, with no context. My argument against:
> Tunde is right about the metric, and I'd be signing up for work my
> current targets punish me for doing.
>
> **Ines:** My argument for keeping it: relationship continuity through
> the first 90 days. My argument against — and this is uncomfortable —
> my reps are compensated on new bookings, so onboarding is the thing
> they do last. I'd be defending a structure my own comp plan
> undermines.
>
> **Dara:** Now mine, and you'll notice it's changed. I came in
> convinced this was a structural question. Three of you have told me
> it's a measurement question — both teams are incentivised against
> doing it well, so moving it just relocates the problem.
>
> **Dara:** Strongest argument against moving it, then. Kwame, you go —
> you're the one who'd gain the team.
>
> **Kwame:** That we spend two quarters on a reorg and land exactly
> where we are, with a worse handover.
>
> **Dara:** That's persuasive. Here's the decision: onboarding moves to
> support from Q3, and it moves with a dedicated onboarding quality
> metric that is not ticket closure time — Bea's score, owned by Kwame,
> reported alongside renewal. Ines, two of your onboarding specialists
> move with it; I'll fund the third from my budget rather than take it
> from your quota headcount. Owner of the transition plan: Kwame, with
> Bea. First draft by the 20th.
>
> **Dara:** Read-back so nobody leaves with a different version. Move to
> support, Q3, new metric replaces closure time for that work, two heads
> move plus one funded by me, Kwame owns the plan, draft on the 20th.
> Anything I've got wrong? ... I'll send that in writing today, and I'll
> talk to the two specialists myself before it's announced.

Twenty-five minutes instead of eight, a materially better decision, and
the person four weeks into the company supplied the insight that changed
it — because he was asked first, before anyone senior had spoken.

## 5. Meetings you attend but don't run

Servant leadership in someone else's meeting is mostly restraint plus
one or two well-placed moves.

- **Give your view late** even when you're not chairing. Your seniority
  anchors regardless of who called the meeting.
- **Redirect credit and questions.** "That analysis is Bea's, and she's
  in the room" costs you nothing and changes what happens next.
- **Name the process, not the content.** "Can I check who's deciding
  this?" is the single most useful sentence available to a participant.
- **Amplify a cut-off point.** "I want to come back to what Rina started
  to say" — a five-word intervention that changes who gets heard.

## Exercise

Take the next meeting you own and fill in the canvas in section 2 before
you send the invite. Force yourself through two boxes honestly: for each
attendee, what happens if they aren't there; and whether the thing is
already decided. If it is, convert the meeting to an inform and say so
out loud — faking a consultation is one of the fastest ways to lose the
trust you built in Level 1.

In the meeting itself, use exactly two moves from section 3: **speak
last** on any question you own, and one of silent start, round, or named
dissent. Keep a private airtime tally as you go. Afterwards, write down
who spoke least, what they said when they finally did, and whether the
outcome differed from what you would have decided in the first two
minutes. If it never differs, you are chairing a ratification process
rather than a decision.
