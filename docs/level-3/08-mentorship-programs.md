# 08 · Mentorship Programs

Informal mentoring — a senior person occasionally helping a junior one
because they happened to click — is real and valuable, and it also
reliably favors whoever is already good at building rapport with power:
people who look like existing leadership, who are naturally outgoing, who
share a hobby with someone senior. A structured mentorship program is how
you make that benefit available on purpose rather than by accident. This
module covers designing a program that produces real development, not a
box-checking pairing exercise that fizzles by month two.

## 1. Why most mentorship programs fail by month two

The typical failure pattern: pairs are announced, given a vague charter
("meet monthly, see what happens"), and left alone. Enthusiasm is high in
month one, meetings get harder to schedule by month three, and the
program dies quietly with nobody willing to declare it over. The common
cause isn't bad matching — it's the absence of structure: no defined
goal per pairing, no cadence enforcement, no way to end a mismatched pair
without it feeling like a personal rejection.

## 2. The Program Design Template

```text
MENTORSHIP PROGRAM DESIGN

PURPOSE (pick one — a program trying to do everything does nothing well)
  [ ] Skill development (specific technical/functional skill)
  [ ] Career navigation (how to grow in this org specifically)
  [ ] Sponsorship (mentor actively advocates for mentee's advancement)
  [ ] Belonging (for underrepresented groups navigating a majority culture)

MATCHING METHOD
  Self-selected from a directory of mentor bios / Assigned by a
  coordinator based on stated goals / Rotating short pairings
  before a longer commitment
  Reasoning for this choice: ______________________________

STRUCTURE
  Duration: ______  Cadence: ______  Format: ______
  Required check-ins with coordinator (not just the pair): ______

EACH PAIR'S OPENING CONTRACT (filled in together, session one)
  Mentee's specific goal for this relationship:
  What mentor can realistically offer (be honest — not everything):
  What "working" looks like at the midpoint check-in:

EXIT RAMP (must exist before launch, not improvised later)
  How either party can end the pairing without it being
  read as a personal failure or public rejection:

PROGRAM-LEVEL SUCCESS MEASURE
  ______________________________________________
```

The exit ramp is the piece programs skip most often, and its absence is
what causes pairs to limp along past the point of usefulness — nobody
wants to be the one who says "this isn't working," so both sides quietly
stop trying instead of formally ending it.

## 3. Worked example: setting the opening contract

> A company launches a sponsorship-focused program pairing senior leaders
> with high-potential mid-level employees. Renata (mentor, a VP) and
> Julio (mentee) have their first session, using the opening contract
> from section 2 instead of an open-ended "get to know you" chat.

> **Renata:** Before we just talk generally — what's the actual goal you
> want this relationship pointed at?
>
> **Julio:** Honestly, I want to be considered for the director role when
> it opens next year, and I don't think I'm visible to the people who'd
> make that call.
>
> **Renata:** That's specific, good. Here's what I can realistically
> offer: I sit in the room where that promotion gets discussed, so I can
> make sure your name and your actual work come up accurately — that's
> sponsorship, not just advice. What I can't do is guarantee the outcome,
> and I won't inflate what you've done to people who'd catch it.
>
> **Julio:** That's more useful than I expected, honestly — I thought
> this would just be career advice.
>
> **Renata:** What does "working" look like for you by the midpoint,
> three months from now?
>
> **Julio:** If my name has come up in at least one conversation about
> that role that I wasn't in the room for, and I know what gap I still
> need to close.
>
> **Renata:** I can commit to that specifically. I'll also tell you
> honestly if I hear the gap is bigger than you think — I won't just
> tell you it's going well if it isn't.

The contract converted a vague "sponsorship program" into a specific,
checkable commitment on both sides, with an honest boundary on what
Renata would and wouldn't do.

## 4. The midpoint check and the exit ramp in practice

A coordinator (not the pair itself) should run a brief midpoint check
with each mentee and mentor separately: "Is this relationship producing
what you hoped, on the goal from your opening contract?" A "no" from
either side triggers a scripted, low-stigma close: "This pairing didn't
find a fit — that happens, and it says nothing about either of you.
Let's find you a different mentor" rather than treating it as a program
failure or a personal one. Programs that build this in from the start see
fewer pairs limping along and more mentees willing to ask for a rematch.

## 5. Group vs. one-on-one mentoring

One-on-one mentoring gives depth but doesn't scale and concentrates
sponsorship power in whoever the mentor happens to be. A mentoring
circle — one senior person, four to six mentees, meeting together —
scales sponsorship further (the mentor can advocate for several people)
and exposes mentees to peers navigating the same stage, which often
produces as much value as the mentor's input. Consider circles for
purposes like career navigation or belonging, and reserve one-on-one for
purposes needing real depth, like targeted skill development.

## 6. Measuring whether the program actually works

Attendance and satisfaction surveys measure whether people liked the
meetings, not whether development happened. Tie the program-level
success measure to the actual purpose chosen in section 2: for
sponsorship, track promotion and stretch-assignment rates for
participants against a comparable non-participant cohort; for skill
development, a before/after skill assessment; for belonging, retention
and internal mobility rates for the target group. A program that scores
well on satisfaction and flat on its actual purpose metric needs
redesign, not a bigger budget.

## Exercise

Design a mentorship program for a specific purpose in your organization
using the template in section 2 — pick one purpose, don't hedge across
all four. Write the opening contract you'd want every pair to fill out in
session one, and write the exact scripted language you'd use for the exit
ramp so it exists before the first pairing launches, not after the first
awkward mismatch.
