# 07 · Ethical Leadership & Decision-Making

Most leadership decisions are not moral dilemmas — they have a clearly
right answer once you gather the facts. This module is about the smaller
set that don't: where two legitimate values genuinely conflict, where
every option costs someone something, and where "what does the data say"
doesn't resolve the question because the question isn't factual. Leaders
who have no framework for these decisions either freeze, defer them
upward reflexively, or rationalize whatever's convenient after the fact.
This module gives you a structured way to work through a genuine ethical
tradeoff and be able to explain your reasoning afterward, even to people
who disagree with the outcome.

## 1. Recognizing a real ethical dilemma

A useful test: if you can resolve the disagreement entirely by getting
more facts, it isn't an ethical dilemma — it's an information gap. A real
dilemma survives full information: even once everyone agrees on the
facts, reasonable people still land in different places because two
things they both value are in tension (loyalty to a struggling employee
vs. fairness to the team covering for them; transparency vs. protecting
someone's dignity; short-term commitments vs. long-term wellbeing).

## 2. The Ethical Decision Framework

```text
ETHICAL DECISION FRAMEWORK

THE DECISION: ________________________________________

STAKEHOLDERS AND WHAT EACH GENUINELY LOSES OR GAINS
  (not just the loudest voice — the quiet ones too)
  ______________________________________________

THE VALUES IN GENUINE TENSION
  (name both sides honestly — if one side sounds like a straw
  man, you haven't found the real tension yet)
  Value A: ____________   Value B: ____________

TEST 1 — TRANSPARENCY: If this decision and my reasoning for
it were made fully public tomorrow, could I defend it, or
would I need to hide the reasoning?
  ______________________________________________

TEST 2 — REVERSAL: If the people affected by this decision
swapped positions with me, would I still think it's the
right call?
  ______________________________________________

TEST 3 — PRECEDENT: If I make this call, and everyone in a
similar position made the same call, is that a world I want?
  ______________________________________________

DECISION: ________________________________________
WHAT I'M KNOWINGLY GIVING UP TO MAKE IT (be specific — a real
ethical decision costs something; if nothing was given up,
recheck whether this was actually a dilemma):
  ______________________________________________
```

The three tests rarely all point the same direction on a genuine
dilemma — that's expected. The framework isn't designed to produce
certainty; it's designed to make sure you've actually reasoned through
the tension instead of picking the option that felt least uncomfortable
in the moment.

## 3. Worked example: a real tradeoff, worked through

> Halima manages a small team. Deshawn, a long-tenured, well-liked
> performer, has been quietly missing deadlines for two months due to a
> family health crisis he hasn't formally disclosed but has hinted at.
> The rest of the team is covering for him and starting to resent it
> without knowing why. Halima has to decide what to do before the
> quarterly review, where his performance will otherwise look like a
> genuine decline.

> Halima works the framework alone before deciding anything.
>
> **Stakeholders:** Deshawn (privacy, dignity, needs support not
> punishment); the team covering for him (fairness, burning out,
> deserve context even if not full details); her own manager (needs an
> honest performance narrative, not a covered-up one); the company
> (consistent standards).
>
> **Values in tension:** Compassion and privacy for Deshawn vs. fairness
> and honesty to the team and to the review process.
>
> **Test 1 (transparency):** Could she defend, publicly, a decision to
> quietly protect Deshawn's rating without telling the team anything?
> Uncomfortable — it would look like favoritism to anyone who didn't
> know the context, and she can't disclose the context without his
> consent.
>
> **Test 2 (reversal):** If she were Deshawn, would she want her
> struggle used to excuse her from ever having the real conversation
> with her manager about what's happening? Probably not — being managed
> around, invisibly, for months, is its own kind of disrespect.
>
> **Test 3 (precedent):** If every manager quietly covered for anyone
> going through something hard, without ever surfacing it, the system
> would have no way to actually support people — it would just look like
> inconsistent performance management to everyone else.
>
> **Decision:** Halima talks to Deshawn directly, not around him. She
> offers to formally flag reduced capacity for the quarter (which
> protects his rating through the actual process, not by hiding it) if
> he's willing to disclose enough to HR to use existing accommodation
> policy — his choice, not hers to force. Separately, she tells the team,
> without medical detail: "Deshawn's carrying something significant right
> now and I've asked HR to look at formal support. I know some of you
> have picked up slack and I see it — here's how I'm redistributing for
> the next six weeks so it's not open-ended."
>
> **What she gave up:** Full privacy protection for Deshawn (he has to
> disclose something to HR to get real accommodation) and the easy option
> of just quietly inflating his rating without addressing the team's
> resentment.

Notice the decision doesn't erase the tension — it manages it
transparently instead of resolving it invisibly, which is usually the
honest outcome of a real ethical dilemma.

## 4. When to escalate rather than decide alone

Escalate when: the decision sets an organization-wide precedent beyond
your team, when you have a personal stake that compromises your
judgment, or when the framework's transparency test fails and you can't
find a version of the decision you could defend publicly. Escalating a
genuine dilemma is not weakness — deciding alone when you shouldn't have
is the actual failure mode.

## 5. A note on speed

Real dilemmas tempt leaders into indefinite deliberation because no
option feels clean. In practice, most ethical decisions have a time cost
to delay that is itself a choice with consequences — the team covering
for Deshawn keeps resenting it the longer nothing changes. Set yourself a
deadline for working through the framework (a day, not a month) and
decide, explicitly accepting the cost identified in the last line of the
framework, rather than treating indecision as neutral.

## Exercise

Identify a real, current decision in your role where two legitimate
values are genuinely in tension — not a fake dilemma with an obvious
right answer. Run the full framework in section 2, including naming what
you're knowingly giving up. If the transparency test fails, don't act
yet — write down specifically what about the reasoning you couldn't
defend publicly, and use that to revise the decision before you make it.
