# 07 · Global Leadership Considerations

Every framework in this track — the delegation levels, direct feedback,
the GROW model's assumption that people will freely disagree with you —
was built inside a particular cultural default, usually a low-context,
individualist, relatively flat-hierarchy assumption about how people
communicate and relate to authority. Leading across cultures means the
same servant-leadership intent can land completely differently, or even
backfire, depending on the cultural context of the person you're leading.
This module is about adapting the mechanics, not abandoning the
intent — the goal (people's growth, genuine listening, empowerment) stays
constant; the specific behavior that achieves it changes.

## 1. Where the track's own tools need adaptation

- **Direct, "radically candid" feedback** (Level 2) assumes the
  recipient reads directness as respect. In higher-context cultures,
  the same bluntness can be read as a public loss of face regardless of
  how privately it's delivered, and can damage trust rather than build
  it.
- **"Speak up in the room" as evidence of psychological safety**
  (Level 2/3) assumes disagreement is best expressed openly and in
  real time. In cultures with stronger hierarchy norms, genuine
  disagreement is more often expressed privately, afterward, or through
  a trusted intermediary — the absence of public pushback does not mean
  the absence of psychological safety, and a leader who only trusts
  the loud, visible form of it will systematically misread quieter
  teams as less safe than they are.
- **The delegation scale's "level 7 — I don't need to know"** (Level 2)
  can read as abandonment rather than trust in cultures where checking
  in frequently is itself a sign of respect and care, not a sign of
  distrust.

## 2. The Cultural Calibration Check

Not a fixed rulebook by country — a discipline for checking your own
default assumption before applying a tool from this track unmodified.

```text
CULTURAL CALIBRATION CHECK

TOOL I'M ABOUT TO USE: ________________________________

MY DEFAULT ASSUMPTION BEHIND IT:
  (e.g., "silence in a meeting means agreement")
  ______________________________________________

WHAT I'D NEED TO CHECK, WITH THE ACTUAL PERSON, BEFORE ASSUMING
MY DEFAULT HOLDS:
  ______________________________________________

A LOWER-RISK WAY TO TEST IT (rather than assuming or asking
"do you disagree with hierarchy" directly, which rarely gets
an honest answer):
  ______________________________________________

WHAT I'LL WATCH FOR AS EVIDENCE, EITHER WAY:
  ______________________________________________
```

The discipline is treating your own communication defaults as one
hypothesis among several, not the ground truth everyone else should be
measured against — including the discomfort of realizing your "more
inclusive" style might read as less respectful to someone raised with a
different norm around directness with someone senior.

## 3. Worked example: reading silence correctly

> Marcus leads a distributed team including Yuki, based in a context
> where direct disagreement with a manager in a group setting is
> uncommon. In a planning meeting, Marcus proposes a technical approach
> and Yuki says nothing. Weeks later the approach fails for a reason
> Yuki had apparently anticipated.

> **Marcus (1:1 afterward, without blame):** I want to understand
> something, not relitigate the decision. When I proposed the caching
> approach in the planning meeting, did you have concerns at the time?
>
> **Yuki:** Yes, actually — I thought the cache invalidation would race
> under load. I didn't say it in the meeting.
>
> **Marcus:** Can I ask why not? I want to understand, not push you to
> justify it.
>
> **Yuki:** Raising a concern with you in front of the whole team felt
> like contradicting you publicly, which isn't something I'm
> comfortable doing, especially on a technical call I wasn't sure I was
> right about.
>
> **Marcus:** That's useful for me to know, and it's on me to build a
> channel that works for you, not to ask you to change how you're
> comfortable raising things. Would it help if I explicitly asked for
> written concerns after every planning meeting, with a day to respond,
> before we finalize an approach?
>
> **Yuki:** That would help a lot, actually — I'll write it down if I
> have a day, even if I wouldn't say it live.
>
> **Marcus:** Then that's a standing part of how I'll run these now, not
> just for you — it'll probably surface things from other people too.

Marcus didn't ask Yuki to become more direct — he built a channel
(written, asynchronous, with a buffer) that matched Yuki's actual
comfort, and made it standard practice rather than a special
accommodation, which also removed any stigma from using it.

## 4. Time, deadlines, and what "urgent" means

Cultures vary genuinely in whether time commitments are treated as
precise contracts or flexible signals of priority — the mismatch shows
up as one side reading the other as either rigid or unreliable, when
both are following a coherent internal logic. The practical fix is
explicit, not assumed: state deadlines with the actual consequence
attached ("this specific date matters because the contract with the
customer is signed against it" versus "this is a target, let's check in
if it slips") rather than relying on a shared, unstated understanding of
what "by Friday" really means.

## 5. What does not get adapted away

Servant leadership's core commitments — genuine listening, real
investment in people's growth, honesty about difficult things — are not
culturally optional, and this module is not license to excuse
disrespect or exploitation as "just a different cultural norm." What
adapts is the specific mechanism (how feedback is delivered, how
disagreement is surfaced, how autonomy is signaled), not whether people
are heard, developed, and treated honestly. A leader who uses "cultural
difference" to justify withholding real feedback or real opportunity
from someone has misapplied this module entirely.

## 6. Building your own cultural range over time

The single most reliable way to build genuine cross-cultural leadership
skill is not reading a general guide to a country's business culture —
it's asking the actual people you lead, individually, how they prefer to
receive feedback, disagree, and be recognized, the way Marcus did with
Yuki. Individual variation within any culture is often larger than the
average difference between cultures; treat every calibration as
person-specific, informed by cultural context but not fully determined
by it.

## Exercise

Pick one tool from earlier in this track — a feedback approach, a
delegation level, a meeting format — that you apply the same way to
everyone on your team regardless of background. Run the Cultural
Calibration Check in section 2 on it for one specific person whose
communication style differs most from your own default, and have the
kind of direct, blame-free conversation Marcus had with Yuki to find out
whether your assumption actually holds for them.
